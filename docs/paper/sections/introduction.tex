\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
A bank that lends money has to decide, for every applicant, whether the
expected gain of a loan outweighs the risk that it will not be repaid. Credit
scoring turns this decision into a supervised classification problem: from
past clients whose outcome is known, estimate the probability that a new
client will default. Two properties matter at the same time. The score has to
\emph{rank} clients well, so that the riskiest applications are the first to
be refused, and it has to be \emph{explainable}, because a refusal often must
be justified to the client or to a regulator \citep{oku2026slides}.

This work uses the public \emph{Give Me Some Credit} dataset
\citep{kaggle2011givemesomecredit} to answer one question: \emph{given a
client's credit history, what is the probability of a serious delinquency
(90 days or more past due) within the next two years?} We compare two methods
from the course that sit at opposite ends of the interpretability--flexibility
trade-off: a regularized logistic regression, whose coefficients read directly
as odds ratios, and a random forest \citep{breiman2001random}, which captures
non-linear effects and interactions without hand-made features. Rather than
only reporting the better score, we measure how much ranking power is given up
by choosing the explainable model.

A second aim is methodological. The data contain impossible values,
informative missing values and a positive class of only 6.7\%, three common
ways of reporting a result that is better than reality. The analysis was
therefore built around the evaluation practices of the course: a test set that
is opened once, every data-dependent step fitted inside cross-validation, and
metrics that a constant prediction cannot game.
